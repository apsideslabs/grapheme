The token got cheaper. The work didn't.

Anthropic cut its output price on Monday and Google opened Gemini 4 Carbon on Tuesday. Both cuts are real. Neither one tells you what a finished task costs.

A Apsides Labs · 12 November 2026 · 7 min read · PRG-2026-010

Two frontier price cuts landed within twenty-four hours of each other this week, and both were announced as good news for anyone paying the bill. Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.2 at $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output tokens, down from $10 and $50 for the previous version. Google opened Gemini 4 Carbon to every paying developer at $2 and $10. Read together, they look like a 20% cut and a 75% gap to the other lab's list.

Neither number is the price of your work. One of them is a discount with an expiry date, and the other belongs to a model that Google itself says spends more tokens to reach an answer. Here is the arithmetic nobody puts in the launch post.

$0.28

What actually shipped

Claude Fable 5.2 (9 November) is the same underlying model as Mythos 5.2, with stronger cyber and biology safeguards, which is why Fable is the version available to everyone. Anthropic says outputs are up to 30% faster than 5.1, and the new price is $8/$40 per million tokens. An optional fast mode runs up to 2.5× faster for $16/$80. Batch requests are half price at $4/$20, and cache reads are $0.20 per million — forty times cheaper than fresh input.

Gemini 4 Carbon (10 November) is Google's frontier model for long-horizon work: multi-step coding, debugging, open-ended research. It is $2/$10 per million tokens until 30 November, then $4/$20. Google says it ranks first on CursorBench 4.0, Perplexity's WANDR and Zapier's AutomationBench, at more than 2× lower cost per task than the next best external model. It also previews /swarm in Antigravity, where one prompt becomes a tracked team of agents.

And on 5 November, OpenAI shipped GPT-6 Cyber to approved customers in its Daybreak Red programme. There is no public price for it, which is its own lesson: the most capable model in the room is increasingly the one you cannot buy by the token.

Worth saying plainly: the benchmark and cost-per-task figures above are the labs' own, from their own launch posts. We have not reproduced them.

Four prices for the same job

Take a concrete workload — a 40,000-token repository context and an 8,000-token answer, about what a mid-sized refactor costs. Price it four ways, using only published rates:

  • Carbon today, 40k in / 8k out. 0.04 × $2 + 0.008 × $10 = $0.16.
  • Carbon today, thinking longer. Google says Carbon uses more tokens than Argon to reason deeply and verify its own work. Give the same task 20k output tokens: 0.04 × $2 + 0.02 × $10 = $0.28 — 75% more, at an unchanged per-token price.
  • Fable 5.2, 40k in / 8k out. 0.04 × $8 + 0.008 × $40 = $0.64. In fast mode, $1.28.
  • Carbon on 1 December. The introductory rate ends on 30 November. Same 40k/20k task at $4/$20: 0.04 × $4 + 0.02 × $20 = $0.56.

So the same piece of work ranges from 16 cents to a dollar twenty-eight depending on which model you point at it and how much thinking you allow, and the cheapest option this week is twice as expensive in three weeks' time. A 20% cut on one list and a 75% gap to the other sit inside a spread that is eight times wide.

Three numbers, and the one that decides

Most cost conversations stop at the first of these:

  1. Price per million tokens. A marketing number. It is the only one that appears in a launch post, and the only one that never appears on your invoice.
  2. Tokens per task. An engineering number. It covers input, reasoning, retries, verification loops and cache misses — and it is the one that moves when a model gets "smarter", usually upward.
  3. Cost per accepted task. The only one that matters. It divides total spend by work you actually kept: the PR that merged, the report that shipped, the question that got answered without a second attempt.

The gap between the first and third is where budgets die. A model that is 4× cheaper per token but needs three attempts and twice the output tokens is not cheaper. A model that is 2× more expensive but lands the task first time often is.

What we measure instead

Our method has not changed since we wrote about evaluating small models honestly, and this week's pricing is exactly why:

  • Fix a task set before you shop. Twenty to fifty real jobs from your own backlog, with a written definition of "accepted". Vendor benchmarks are someone else's backlog.
  • Count tokens, including the retries. Log input, output and cache behaviour per attempt. The second attempt is part of the price of the first.
  • Price at the post-promotion rate. Budget Carbon at $4/$20 today, not $2/$10. Introductory pricing is a loan against next quarter's margin.
  • Structure prompts for the cache. At $0.20 against $8.00 per million input tokens, a stable prefix is a 40× discount on everything it covers. That is a larger saving than most model swaps.
  • Batch everything that can wait. Half price on both labs' sheets, and most analysis, backfills and evaluations can wait.
How we think about it: the cheapest token we run is the one on the laptop in front of us. We keep models local and CPU-only wherever the task allows, so the marginal cost of a thought is electricity rather than a metered API — and the bill does not change when a lab ends its introductory period.

Both cuts this week were real, and both are worth taking. But a price list is a menu, not a bill. The number you will actually pay is decided by how many tokens you let the model spend, how many attempts you accept, and whether you priced the discount or the deadline.
